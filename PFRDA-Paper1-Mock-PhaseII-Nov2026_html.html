Q2. Précis

30 marks

Make a précis of the following passage in 140–160 words and give it a suitable title.

Every economy rests on work that rarely appears in its accounts. Cooking, cleaning, fetching water, looking after children, nursing the sick and caring for elderly parents keep households, and through them the workforce, running. Economists call this the care economy. Part of it is paid, as with nurses, domestic workers, anganwadi workers and crèche staff, but the larger part is unpaid and done at home, overwhelmingly by women. Because no money changes hands, this work is left out of GDP, and what is not counted is easily ignored in policy.

The imbalance in India is stark. The national Time Use Survey of 2019 found that women spent about five hours a day on unpaid domestic work, while men spent about an hour and a half. This burden is one of the main reasons why India's female labour force participation, though rising in recent years, remains well below that of many countries at a similar level of income. A woman who must care for a toddler and an ailing parent has little time left for a paid job, training or a business of her own, and when she does work, it is often in informal, part-time or home-based jobs with low pay and no social security.

The demand for care is also set to grow. India's population is ageing, families are becoming smaller, and more young people are moving to cities for work, leaving older parents behind. The old assumption that a daughter or daughter-in-law will provide care at no cost is wearing thin. Without new arrangements, the pressure will fall either on women, who will be pushed out of paid work, or on the elderly and children, who will go without adequate care.

Investing in care is not only a social obligation but sound economics. Several international studies suggest that public spending on childcare and long-term care creates more jobs for every rupee spent than spending on construction, and most of these jobs go to women. Affordable crèches free mothers to join the workforce; trained care workers improve child nutrition and early learning; and support for the elderly at home reduces the pressure on hospitals. In this sense, care is infrastructure, as essential to growth as roads and power lines.

The obstacles are real. Paid care workers, from domestic helpers to anganwadi staff, are often poorly paid and lack formal contracts. Public childcare is scarce, especially in cities, and the quality of private crèches varies widely. Social norms that treat care as "women's work" discourage men from sharing it at home.

The way forward lies in recognising, reducing and redistributing care work. Counting unpaid work through regular time-use surveys would make it visible. Expanding anganwadis into full-day crèches, and enforcing the legal duty of larger employers to provide crèche facilities, would lighten the load on mothers. Fair wages, training and social security would turn paid care into decent work. Finally, paternity leave and public campaigns can encourage men to share the work at home. An economy that values care will be both fairer and more productive.

Q3. Reading comprehension

40 marks · 10 each

Read the passage below and answer the questions based on it in your own words.

Traditional economics assumed that people are rational: they weigh costs and benefits, look ahead and choose what is best for them. Real people behave differently. They put off decisions, stay with whatever option is already in place, copy what those around them do and value the present far more than the future. Behavioural economics, which grew out of the work of psychologists and economists from the 1970s onward, studies these patterns. Its best-known policy idea is the "nudge", made famous by Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein in their 2008 book of that name. Thaler went on to receive the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2017.

A nudge is any feature of the way a choice is presented that changes behaviour in a predictable way without forbidding any option or significantly changing financial incentives. Placing fruit at eye level in a school canteen is a nudge; banning junk food is not. Whoever designs the setting in which people choose is a "choice architect", and the central insight is that there is no neutral design. Every form, menu or website steers people one way or another, so it may as well steer them towards choices they would themselves endorse.

The most powerful tool is the default, the option that applies if a person does nothing. When the United Kingdom began enrolling employees automatically in workplace pension schemes from 2012, while leaving them free to opt out, the share of eligible private-sector employees who saved for retirement rose from well under half to a large majority within a few years. Nothing about the financial rewards of saving had changed; only the starting point had. Because people tend to stay with the default, making the good choice the automatic one turns inertia from an enemy into an ally.

Governments have taken note. The United Kingdom set up a Behavioural Insights Team in 2010, and many countries followed. India's Economic Survey of 2018-19 devoted a chapter to the subject, citing campaigns such as Swachh Bharat, Beti Bachao Beti Padhao and the "Give It Up" appeal, under which crores of households voluntarily surrendered their LPG subsidy. Small changes, such as tax reminders that mention that most people in the town have already paid, or electricity bills that compare a household's use with that of its neighbours, have changed behaviour at a fraction of the cost of new laws.

Nudges have their critics, however. Some argue that they are manipulative, since they work by exploiting the very biases they claim to correct, often without people noticing. Others ask who decides what is "good" for citizens, and fear that the same techniques can be used by companies to push people into subscriptions, loans or purchases they do not need, the so-called "dark patterns" of online design. A third criticism is that nudges are too small for big problems: no amount of clever framing will close a pension gap if wages are too low to save, or cut carbon emissions without pricing and regulation.

The fair conclusion is that nudges are a complement to conventional policy, not a substitute for it. They work best when the goal is one that people already share, when the nudge is transparent and easy to refuse, and when it is tested carefully before being scaled up. Used in that spirit, they can make public policy cheaper, more humane and more effective. Used as a replacement for hard choices about taxes, spending and rules, they risk becoming an excuse for doing too little.
